My first personal computer was an IBM PS/2 Model 80. It had an Intel 486 running at a blistering 25 MHz, 8 MB of RAM, and a whopping 160 MB hard drive. It played *Wolfenstein* like a champ. For my birthday in 1995, I bought myself the Toshiba Satellite Pro T2150CDT. It had an Intel 486DX4 at 75 MHz, 4 MB of RAM, a 500 MB hard drive, and the first laptop with a built-in CD player. After that, I never went back to a desktop again.

Portability was the key. If I could play *Wolfenstein* in a dentist’s waiting room, why would I chain myself to a desk at home? The world was at my fingertips, why limit where I could access it?

Today’s Gen Z seems to agree. **98% own a smartphone**, **83% own a laptop**, and only **50% own a desktop**. When I asked a few Gen Z coworkers what their “main computer” was, every single one pointed to their phone. One even said she hadn’t opened her laptop in years.
“I can literally do everything I need on my phone,” she told me.

Could *I* do everything I need on a phone? I wanted to find out.

My privacy-hardened GrapheneOS phone wasn’t a good candidate for the experiment, so I picked up an iPhone 17 Pro Max (mostly for the battery life), a Satechi 2-in-1 Foldable Wireless Charging Stand, and a Satechi Slim X1 Bluetooth Keyboard. I still can’t believe this iPhone costs more than a MacBook Pro.

I followed the setup instructions from [*Extreme Privacy*](https://inteltechniques.com/book7.html). Then I made a list of the apps I rely on most on my laptop: Standard Notes, KeePassXC, Newsflash, Firefox, Calibre, Podcasts, and a simple movie/music player like VLC.

Here’s where I landed for iOS equivalents:

- Standard Notes > Standard Notes
- KeePassXC > Strongbox
- Newsflash > Feedly
- Firefox > Orion
- Calibre > Apple Books
- Podcasts > Apple Podcasts
- VLC > VLC

The keyboard turned out to be a game changer, especially when writing in Standard Notes. Paired with the charging stand, it made the setup surprisingly productive. Sure, it’s technically cheating, most Gen Zers aren’t docking their phones at home, but it made the experiment far more enjoyable.

And here’s the surprising part: I can actually see myself getting used to this setup. For at-home use, it’s completely viable. But the moment I have to carry a keyboard and stand outside the house, a laptop suddenly becomes the more practical option. Considering the price of the iPhone, though, I can see why younger generations stick to a single device. You *can* do almost everything on it, it’s ultra-portable, and you really can carry your entire digital life in your pocket.

Which brings me to the downsides.

Aside from the fact that a standard smartphone is a privacy nightmare, you still need a computer to properly back it up. When I asked my coworkers about their backups, the most common answer I heard was, “I think it automatically backs up to the cloud.”
What they didn’t realize is that unless you configure it, all their documents, files, music, and movies *aren’t* actually backed up. When your whole digital life lives in your pocket, a solid backup strategy is mandatory.

The other challenge was multitasking. I frequently bounce between a notes app, a browser, the Files app, and a handful of PDFs. You *can* do that on an iPhone, but it takes practice, and the workflow never feels quite as fluid as a laptop.

Overall, the experiment was costly, but enjoyable. There’s something genuinely freeing about having your entire digital life with you wherever you go. And despite what older generations might think, Gen Z understands this intuitively. Their entire lives have been online; constant connectivity is their baseline. The idea of not having that access feels foreign to them.

Just like privacy.

So yes, *I* can realistically run my digital life from a phone, but doing so exposed how little people understand about privacy and backups.
